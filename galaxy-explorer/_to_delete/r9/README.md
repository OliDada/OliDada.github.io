# Elsewhere — procedural worlds

A standalone procedural 3D exploration prototype for olid.is. New code and procedural assets; no Solar System game code is reused.

## Run

From the website repository: `python3 -m http.server 8765`, then open `http://localhost:8765/galaxy-explorer/`. Static HTML and modules; no build step. Three.js 0.161.0 is vendored with its MIT license. Fonts have system fallbacks.

## Explore

- **M** opens the star map on your current system; **G** opens it on your stellar neighbourhood. It is one full-screen map: zoom (scroll, pinch, or +/−) from the whole spiral galaxy down through arms and neighbourhoods to a single system's planets and moons. Tap to select, double-tap a star or world to fly to it. The side panel (a bottom sheet on phones) shows the selection, the selected system's worlds, and stars in jump range.
- From the map, **Cruise** to a world in your system, or **Jump** to a star within 12 light years. Jumping needs free space (above 600 m); if a star is isolated in a gap, the drive overcharges just enough to reach the nearest neighbour. Systems you have not visited show only orbits until you jump there.
- **T** engages cruise to the selected destination or disengages an ongoing cruise into manual flight.
- Every star in the sky is a real star you can fly to. Fly past the outer orbits and the star drive engages: **W/S** set the throttle, **A/D** and **↑/↓** steer, **Shift** boosts. Choose a star on the galaxy map and **Set course**; **T** then toggles the autopilot. Nearing a star builds its system in the background, and you fly straight in at its edge.
- Click the view for mouse look (Esc releases).
- **Targets:** tap or click a world, moon or star in the view (or press **Tab** to cycle, nearest the middle of the view first). Its name and distance show on the waypoint, and in space the ship keeps its nose on it whenever you are not steering; steering takes over until you let go. Tab starts with the nearest world, then cycles through what is in view. Tapping empty space, the **Clear target** button or **C** clears it, leaving no destination at all. Stars can be picked from inside a system too: the marker then points the way to that star with its distance in light years, the ship turns toward it, and **T** engages an autopilot that flies you out of the system and on to the star. Setting a course always replaces a world you had in focus. In deep space, picking a star sets your course; picking a world of the system ahead sets your course to that world, which becomes your target on arrival.
- **W/S** thrust, **A/D** turn, **↑/↓** pitch, **R/F** vertical thrusters, **Shift** boost, **X** brake.
- Climb above **600 m** to leave local orbit. Lift gets stronger the higher you are, so the climb takes seconds, and the view eases from looking down at the ground to looking ahead. In free space, movement is unrestricted; entering a world's vicinity restores surface flight. The star has an exclusion zone; well before it the hull heats up, the view glows at the edges and warnings appear.
- **L** begins a 3.8-second landing below 32 m and 18 m/s forward speed. On the ground it begins a 3.6-second powered liftoff to 22 m. Both use gear, lift jets, and dust.
- **E** disembarks, investigates within 9 m of a signal, or boards within 9 m of the ship.
- On foot: **WASD** walk, mouse or arrow keys look, **Space** jump, **Shift** walk faster. You can wade into seas up to about chest-deep (slower the deeper it gets); with the Dive suit you swim on past that (see Water).
- The Petrel under water (Pressure hull I): fly low over deep water and hold **F**, or press **F** while afloat, to dive. **W/S** throttle, **A/D** or the mouse turn, **R/F** rise and sink, **X** stop, **E** scan (or, with the Dive suit, swim out). Hold **R** at the surface to fly out again.
- **H** opens the manual and pauses. Sound is opt-in. Touch movement and contextual action buttons are included.

## Generation and persistence

Worlds are generated from physics, not from a fixed list. Each step only reads the one before it:

1. **Star** (`physics.mjs`): spectral class drawn by real-ish frequency (half are red dwarfs). Mass gives luminosity, temperature, colour, lifespan, flare activity, habitable zone and frost line.
2. **Planets and moons**: 4–7 planets on roughly geometric orbits, plus 0–2 moons per planet (9 bodies max). Orbit distance and luminosity give temperature; mass gives radius, gravity and escape velocity; gravity, heat and flares decide how much atmosphere survives; greenhouse warming, water state (liquid, ice, steam, subsurface ocean), tidal locking and volcanism follow. About two-thirds of systems get a gameplay nudge that places one Earth-sized planet in the habitable zone.
   **Giants**: each planet may instead be a gas or ice giant. They are most likely beyond the frost line (more so around heavier stars), less likely just inside it, and rare close in (hot giants). Mass (12–3000 Earth masses) sets the kind: ice giants below ~50, gas giants above, all about Jupiter's size and puffed up by strong starlight. Starlight plus leftover internal heat give the cloud-top temperature, which picks the clouds: ammonia (tan bands), methane (blue-green), water (white), clear (deep blue), alkali (dark) or silicate (glowing red). Giants always have 1–4 moons, often tidally heated, and often rings. About 43% of systems have at least one.
3. **Habitability and life**: liquid water, mild temperature, workable pressure and gravity, star age and calm all multiply into a score; the score decides the chance and level of life (none, microbial, simple, complex).
   **Life on other chemistry** (`exoticChemistry` in `physics.mjs`): worlds without liquid water can still host life built on something else. Cold worlds with thick air get **ammonia** seas (about −80 to −20 °C), very cold ones **methane** lakes like Titan (around −175 °C) with an orange haze, hot volcanic worlds **sulfur** life (and molten-sulfur pools), and very hot worlds **silicon** life. Each has its own habitability score, scaled down because these are long shots, and needs an older star to reach complex life. It is drawn from the same random numbers as before, so no other world changed. About a fifth of worlds with animals run on exotic chemistry. Exotic life has its own plants, animal palettes and slower metabolism (methane life is very slow), shows a violet ring and an EXOTIC tag on the star map, and is labelled as, for example, "complex life (methane-based)".
4. **Biome and look** (`biomes.mjs`): Volcanic, Toxic, Desert, Verdant, Oceanic, Frozen, Amethyst or Barren, chosen from the physics. Each world gets a seeded hue shift; vegetation is tinted by the star (dark red-purple under red dwarfs, pale under hot stars); lower gravity raises relief. Descriptions and map facts are written from the actual numbers. Sunlight takes the star's colour.

Randomness is hash-based (`rng.mjs`): every object draws from its own key path, such as `rng(seed, 'planet', 3)`, so any object can be generated alone and new traits don't reshuffle existing worlds. Real units live in `body.physics`; game distances and sizes are compressed.

Enter a seed in the map or use **Discover a new system**. The seed is in the URL (`?seed=1408`), so links reproduce a system. Generating restarts flight; visits and recorded signals are stored separately per seed. Reloading returns you to your current star, above its most habitable world. The log button opens the current world's recorded note.

`node galaxy-explorer/inspect.mjs 1408 42` prints systems as tables; `node galaxy-explorer/inspect.mjs --stats 3000` prints star, biome and life frequencies for tuning.

## Galaxy

`galaxy.mjs` defines a two-to-four-armed spiral galaxy 60,000 light years across (seed `GALAXY_SEED`). Nothing is stored: space is cut into 10-light-year cells and each cell's stars come from `hash(galaxySeed, cell)`. A density field (exponential disc, logarithmic spiral arms, central bulge, thin vertical profile) sets each cell's expected star count, so arms, sparse gaps and a crowded core emerge on their own. A star's system is `generateSystem(star.seed)`; star ids encode their cell (`?star=-917_1262_0_0`), so any star can be rebuilt from a link. `starmap.js` draws one continuous map, generating detail for the current zoom so nothing is ever a stretched image: a soft haze of the whole galaxy, then procedural light points from a quadtree of tiles (`visualTile`, one level finer each time the view halves), then real stars from the cells in view, then each star's orbits, planets and moons. Systems are drawn compressed (outer orbit ≈ 0.3 ly) so you can zoom straight into them. Nothing is redrawn unless the view changes, the 3D scene pauses while the map is open, and the map avoids CSS backdrop blur, which made each frame several times slower. Stars are thinned to roughly a fifth of real density near home so the local chart stays readable. The chart is seen from above, but jump range is measured in 3D: stars far above or below you are drawn smaller and fainter (unless in range), and labels, the jump list and the side panel give each star's height, such as "6 ly below".

## Interstellar flight

The sky is the galaxy's real stars (`deep-space.js`): every star within 60 light years is drawn in its true direction, sized and brightened by its luminosity over distance squared, over a faint backdrop of unresolved stars. Inside a system you see them from its star; in deep space, from wherever you are, so they shift as you travel.

A system's edge sits just beyond its outermost orbit. Systems are compressed, so each edge counts as 0.002 light years from its star. Past it, the ship's position is kept in light years and the scene stays centred on it. The star drive covers the distance to the nearest star in about 5 seconds at full throttle (2 with boost), so speed grows far from stars and falls near them: a 5-light-year trip takes about a minute, and arrivals are gentle. Light comes from the nearest star, the star ahead grows into a sphere sized to match what you will see from its edge, and streaks show the drive at work.

Within 0.4 light years of a star its system is built in the background and its real worlds are drawn as a miniature: scaled down around the camera so it fits within view range, yet looking exactly like the full-size system from where you are. Worlds still under two pixels across show as dots so the system is visible from afar. This covers the system you are leaving as well as the one ahead. The system is built, one world per frame and giant cloud maps in slices, so arriving never stalls the game. At the edge you enter it on the side you came from, still heading the same way; your galaxy position, the URL and the map switch to the new star. The autopilot flies past stars that are not its destination; flying in by hand enters any system. Off-chart test systems (`?seed=`) have no place in the galaxy, so their edge is closed.

Your current star, visited stars and jump count are saved in `localStorage` (`elsewhere-galaxy-v1`). Jumps play a warp effect and reload into the destination system; after a jump you start in open space a couple of kilometres from a world, on its sunlit side, facing it. `?seed=` still opens a standalone off-chart system for testing; jumps from one are measured from your last charted star.

## Terrain

`terrain.mjs` builds each world's surface from seeded 3D simplex noise sampled on the sphere, so there are no seams or poles. Each biome has its own shape: continents with mountain ridges, wind-aligned dunes on deserts, cracked plains on frozen worlds, sharp crystal ridges on amethyst worlds, cones and calderas on volcanic worlds, and impact craters on airless bodies.

Sea level comes from the physics. Ocean coverage (for example 65%) is turned into a height, by sampling the whole world and picking the level that floods that share. Liquid-water worlds get transparent oceans with visible shallows, volcanic worlds get glowing lava seas, and frozen worlds get flat ice sheets you can land and walk on. Colour is chosen per point from altitude, latitude, local temperature (colder toward poles and peaks) and a moisture field: beaches along coasts, vegetation tinted by the star where there is plant-like life, darker forests where it is wet, microbial mats near shore, snow caps, lava-crust shores. Rocks take a darker shade of the ground they sit on.

**Mountains** (new land): massifs a kilometre or few across rise as a swell out of the land, and on them ridges branch off main crests (ridged noise on a warped domain, each octave weighted by the one before, so fine ridges and gullies sit on the high ground and valley floors stay smooth). Crests are rounded off by about a mesh spacing, so they never zig-zag along the grid of vertices. High up, rock shows through on gentler slopes too, in pale grey mountain rock with wandering strata, with scree gathered in the hollows below the crags and short golden alpine grass above the woods; peaks are about 40 K colder than the coast. Rivers rise in the hills (none on the high ground) and their courses have few closed loops. On coarser (more distant) terrain tiles, features need 8 rather than 6 vertex spacings to show, which removed waffle-like ripples on long slopes.

Ground cover (new plant style) is a thicker carpet out to 58 m (32 m on phones): grass in clumps of several tufts, some taller and some dry, and on bare ground gravel round the stones, scattered grit and flat stones half sunk in the ground. Plants' and animals' surface grain is lighter and fixed to their rest shape, so it no longer slides as they sway, and foliage colours are a little muted so plants sit with the ground.

Beaches (new land) blend into the land: how far up the sand reaches wanders along the coast, sand gives way to grass raggedly, and steep banks get none (a fixed height threshold drew blocky, stepped edges). Small moons get gentle, rounded country (ranges and mesas scaled down below about 1.8 km radius). Loose rocks are filled out to the trees' reach (pebble-sized ones only within 300 m) and thin out at the edge like plants. The close-up ground detail covers a wide area round you (out to 120-260 m; 45-110 m on phones), each layer still fading once smaller than a pixel.

Far ground detail (`planet-lod.js`): beyond the close-up grain, the ground shader adds broad patches a few metres to a hundred across (lighter and darker ground, drier and lusher grass), each fading only once smaller than a few pixels, so distant country never looks like a smooth wash; tiles also split a little earlier (2.1 × their width).

**Landforms** (the new land style, `LANDFORMS` in `terrain.mjs`; `?land=classic` brings back the old gentler terrain). On top of the shapes above, every world is built from a few kinds of country, each on its own noise and random stream (`'landform'`), so nothing else about a world changed:

- **Continents** rise from the sea over a shelf. A low-frequency, warped field decides where land is, levelled by its own quantile so the share of land is known before anything else is added; the land steps up just inland of the coast and the sea floor drops away beyond it, so land comes in a few big connected masses instead of scattered specks. Ocean worlds keep real continents to walk across: seas over three fifths of the surface are only partly drawn as sea (97% becomes 77%), so a typical ocean world now has a quarter of its surface dry, most of it in one or two landmasses. Island chains dot some coasts.
- **Hills**: rolling country (some regions hilly, some flat plain), gentle near the coast.
- **Mountain ranges** along narrow belts, highest far inland: a couple of hundred metres on an Earth-sized world, less on small moons, more under weak gravity, with foothills around them. The tallest peaks are about 38 K colder than the coast (the lapse rate follows the world's relief), so mild worlds get snow caps and a tree line.
- **Rivers** (on most wet worlds): winding along the zero line of their own field, so they meander, join the sea and never end in a wall. Across a river: the water channel (its width varying along the course), a flat floodplain, then valley sides easing back up into the land, wider in high country. In the lowlands the channel lies below sea level, so the sea runs up it as a river; high in the hills the bed climbs with the land and runs dry. Deserts get dry canyons.
- **Mesas**: flat-topped tablelands with cliff edges and a terraced fringe, on deserts above all (every desert has mesas or canyons), and on some toxic, volcanic and crystal worlds.
- Volcanoes and craters are scaled up with the world.

Each world varies these by its own roll: some are gentle plains, some alpine. About half of the land is gentler than 10°, a tenth steeper than 32°. The colour ramp is eased so the lowlands under the tall ranges keep their variety.

The terrain is never flattened: the signal site is simply the nearest dry, fairly level ground to the generated signal point, found by a spiral search, so it is never under water or lava. Kestrel cannot land in water or lava (the Petrel can float on water), and you cannot walk into them. Add `&world=planet-1` (or `moon-0`, etc.) to a URL to start above a specific world.

## Plant life

Living worlds get their own species (`flora.mjs`): 1–2 microbial forms (mats, layered mounds) on microbial worlds, 3–5 species with simple life, 5–8 with complex life. Each world's environment decides which forms thrive: broad-canopied trees, tiered spires, fronds, bushes, grasses, water-hoarding pillars, stalked caps or pod-stalks. Temperature and water favour some forms over others. Gravity sets height (low gravity grows tall, thin plants; high gravity keeps them squat). The star's light sets leaf colour, from green under Sun-like stars to near-black and magenta under red dwarfs. Dim light makes bioluminescence common. Exotic worlds grow their own forms instead: glassy crystal clusters and branching glasswood (silicon), mineral chimneys crowned with feeding tubes and feathery plumes (sulfur), waxy bulbs and translucent sails (ammonia), and towering beaded threads and tar-dark crusts (methane). Every species has a generated name, colours, shape and a preferred habitat (temperature, moisture, shoreline or highland), and is described from those traits.

Models have real detail: trees have root flares, bark rings, twigs and broken-up crowns with fruit; conifers have drooping bough skirts; palms have ringed trunks and long fronds that droop and fold along the midrib; shrubs have several stems, twigs and berries; grasses have arching flat blades and seed heads; pillar plants have ribs, rows of spines and a crowning flower; caps have gills, a stem ring and spots; pod-stalks have leaves at the nodes. `flora-mesh.js` builds one low-poly model per species and draws every plant of that species as an instance, which keeps it to a couple of draw calls per species. **Plants grow all over the world, evenly**, not just at the landing site: the world is divided into a fixed grid of 12 m cells (`scatter.js`, one grid per cube face, thinned towards the cube corners so density stays even), and each cell works out from its own hash and its climate what grows in it. Only cells near you are filled and drawn (320 m on desktop, 220 m on touch devices; small plants only within about half that), a few per frame and nearest first, and plants thin out smoothly at the edge of view, so nothing pops in. Up to about 3,200 plants (1,800 on touch devices) and a million triangles (450k) are drawn at once. Walk away and come back and every plant is where it was. **Plant biomes** (`plantBiome` in `flora.mjs`): each spot is forest, grassland, wetland (low, wet ground by a shore), dry scrub, highland or barren (too cold or too hot for the world's life), from its moisture, temperature and altitude, which vary across a world in broad regions. A biome sets how thick the growth is (forest and wetland thick, scrub and highland sparse, barren nearly bare) and which forms are at home there (trees, spires and glasswood in forests; grasses, bulbs and plumes in grassland; fronds, mats and mounds in wetlands; pillars, crystals, chimneys and crusts in dry scrub; spires and bushes in highland); each species' own habitat then decides exactly where it grows, and species grow in groves and patches tens of metres across rather than evenly mixed. Plants never grow under water, at the site itself or on the landing pad. They sway in the wind, more in thick air and less in thin. Flat growths (microbial mats, crusts) lie along the slope, in overlapping patches of slightly different thickness that reach into the ground, so they never flicker or float. **Individuality.** Each species also has a character of its own (`traitsFor` in `flora.mjs`, on its own random stream so older worlds keep their species): a growth habit (straight, leaning, twisted, or clumps of several stems), bark (plain, banded, pale with dark marks, mottled), a weeping crown, trailing ivy-like vines (likelier on wet worlds), and blossom, hanging fruit, berries, a crowning flower or spores in a bloom colour of its own, with a share of plants in bloom at any time. The description says so ("They grow twisted, with pale bark and trailing vines. Many are in blossom."). Within a species no two plants are clones: each species has up to three models with different branching, lean and twist (one of them in bloom), and every plant has its own age (young saplings and a few old giants), proportions (broader or slimmer) and a slightly warmer or cooler colour. Scan a plant (see Scanning) to catalogue its species; catalogued species are saved per world and listed on the star map.

**Forests** (the new plant style, `?plants=classic` for the old models and spacing). Forests are made to feel like woods you could get lost in:

- **Where**: the plant biomes gain a **darkwood**, the dark old heart of a forest where it is wettest (`plantBiome` in `flora.mjs`). Forests are commoner (about a third of the land on living worlds), with ragged edges and **glades**, from a stand field in the climate (`stand`, a hundred metres or two across) that the trees, the ground colour and the air all read.
- **How thick**: trees take a jittered grid of four spots in each 12 m cell, 6 m apart, so they stand close but always at least about 3 m apart, room to walk between: a tree every 36-40 m² in darkwood, a little fewer in forest, a scattered few in meadows. Crowns sit high on long bare trunks, as in an old forest. Smaller plants fill in between, fewer under a closed canopy. Most trees are grown; saplings come up where the stand thins at a glade's edge, and old giants are commonest in darkwood.
- **Every world's own trees** (`shapeFor` and `SHAPES` in `flora.mjs`, built by `alienTree` in `flora-forest.js`, on their own random stream `'flora-shape'`): each tree and spire species is a **trunk plan** (straight column, swollen bottle, upturned candelabra arms, braided stems, stilt roots, a bowing arch, ringed segments, forking again and again, or a corkscrew) and a **crown plan** (stacked flat discs, lumpy globes, a flat umbrella canopy, weeping strands, a spiral of leaves, bristling bottlebrush, stiff fans of fronds, hanging lantern pods, a crown that widens upward, puffs on thin stalks, shelf-like plates, or streaming ribbons), with its own girth, crown height, spread, layer count, lean, droop and lumpiness, and sometimes thorns or seed pods. Its description says which ("A trunk held up on stilt roots, hung with swollen lantern-like pods."). Familiar Earth-like trees grow only where a world is much like Earth (a Sun-like star, mild temperatures, about Earth's gravity), and even there only about half the time.
- **Earth-like trees** (`flora-forest.js`): trees and spires grow far taller (conifers 1.75×, trees 1.45×, up to 28 m). Conifers are dark **firs** (a bare lower trunk with dead branch stubs, buttress roots running into the ground, a jagged skirt of drooping boughs tier on tier up to a narrow spire) or tall **pines** with a reddening trunk and a crown only near the top; broadleaf trees are heavy **oaks** (buttress roots, a short thick bole splitting into great limbs, a lumpy crown with a shadowed underside) or, with pale bark, slim **birches** with airy crowns. The classic models are all still in `flora-mesh.js`.
- **The floor**: dark moss and fallen needles and leaves instead of meadow (`terrain.mjs`), drifts of **ferns**, moss cushions, fallen twigs and small mushrooms (`groundcover.js`), grass shaded out, and **deadwood**: fallen trunks, broken stumps and moss-grown boulders in the bark of the world's own trees and the colour of its rock, which you bump into.
- **The air** (`updateLocalAir` in `game.js`): under the trees of a darkwood the light goes dim and green and the distance closes into haze; an ordinary forest is shaded and a little hazy; glades let the light back in. Other country has its own air too: wetlands lie under a low mist, dry scrub and deserts shimmer with dust, frozen ground has a white ice haze, volcanic country an ash haze and toxic country a yellow one. It eases in and out over a couple of seconds as you walk, and fades as you climb away from the ground.
- **How far**: trees are filled on a grid of their own out to 720 m (420 m on phones), thinning towards the horizon, so hills are wooded as far as you can see and from the air; small plants keep the old reach. Trees use their lighter model beyond 45 m (30 m) and a **distant** model beyond 170 m (110 m): the tree itself simplified hard, its foliage gathered into a few masses in its own colours, so it keeps its silhouette. Up to about 9,600 trees (2,700 on phones) and 1.1 million triangles for trees, plus 0.75 million for the rest.
- **Wind**: big trees barely move: only the top stirs, slowly, so a forest stands still in a breeze.

## Geology

Every world with a surface has geology to find (`geology.mjs`), chosen from its physics, so even a lifeless moon is worth landing on.

**Rocks**, 2–3 types on every world, look like natural boulders, layered outcrops, rubble and ice:

- **Volcanic:** basalt, obsidian, green olivine rock.
- **Airless:** pale anorthosite and impact breccia.
- **Where water once lay:** sandstone, shale, limestone (built from shells where there is sea life), rust-red ironstone, white gypsum.
- **Elsewhere:** granite, water ice on frozen worlds, sulfur crusts on toxic and sulfur worlds.

**Landforms** are rarer, on about 2 worlds in 5:

- **Volcanic:** basalt columns and fumaroles.
- **Desert:** hoodoos and rock arches.
- **Frozen:** penitentes, pingos and cryovolcanoes.
- **Amethyst and silicon worlds:** crystal spires.
- **Toxic and sulfur worlds:** mineral terraces and sulfur chimneys.
- **Wet worlds:** karst pinnacles and sea stacks.
- **Anywhere:** rare geodes and iron meteorites.

Only vents and chimneys glow. Weak gravity makes everything taller. `geology-mesh.js` spreads each kind thinly over the whole world on dry ground, with more on bigger worlds: up to 60 of a rock type and 24 of a landform. Two of each rock type and one of each landform sit within 150 m of the landing site, so every landing has something to scan. High landforms take the highest ground, coastal ones sit by the water. Each kind is one instanced mesh, plus one for any glowing parts. Scan rocks and landforms to record them (both count as Geology). They are solid on foot, and settlements clear them from their ground.

## Animal life

Worlds with simple or complex life get animals (`fauna.mjs`): 1–3 small crawlers, skitterers, creepers or coils with simple life, 4–8 species with complex life. Each species is generated in four layers:

1. **Body plan and form**, chosen by the environment: four-legged (runner, tank, browser, prowler, hopper), two-legged (strider, raptor, waddler, upright), six-legged (beetle, mantis, ant, roach), eight-legged (spider, harvestman, scorpion), legless coil (worm, eel, segmented), slug or snail, winged glider (bat, insect, bird), or gas-filled drifter (jellyfish bell, ribbed balloon, manta, bladder cluster). Weak gravity favours fliers, drifters and long legs; nothing flies in near-vacuum.
2. **Traits** (the species' stats, all 0–1 except diet): diet (herbivore, omnivore, carnivore, filter-feeder), intelligence, sociality, aggression, caution, metabolism, perception. Intelligence is rare and grows with the biosphere's age.
3. **Everything else derives from plan + traits + environment.** Hunters get forward-facing eyes and long snouts; grazers get side eyes and longer necks; defensive grazers grow horns; bigger brains mean bigger heads; clever bipeds stand upright and get arms with hands. Herd size follows sociality, speed follows legs and metabolism, and reactions to you follow caution, aggression and intelligence (bolting, sizing you up, curiosity). A **civilization level** (none, tool users, village builders, metalworkers, city builders, industrial, planet-shapers, orbital, spacefarers, system-shapers) comes from intelligence, sociality, how well the body can shape things, and the star's age (see Intelligence).

   Two body plans have no counterpart among Earth's land animals: **radial** walkers (a three-legged tripod with its body slung high and an eye stalk on top, a five-armed starwalker that sees from its arm tips, an octopus-like tentacle walker), which have no head and no front, and **rollers** (a living wheel rolling on a muscular rim around an upright, eyed hub, and an armoured ball that rolls with eyes at the ends of its axle).
4. **Look**, mixed and matched per species so no two worlds share an animal: colour scheme (camouflage matched to the biome, warning colours, iridescent, display), pattern (bands, stripes, rings, spots, mottled, dapple, saddle, gradient), head shape (round, long, wedge, flat, domed, beaked), jaws and teeth, brows, ears, antennae, mandibles, neck frills, back (shell, spines, plates, hump, sail, ridge), tail (whip, fan, club, spiked, scorpion stinger), feet (claws, hooves, pads), eye count, and on dark worlds glowing markings. Chemistry sets the palette on exotic worlds: glassy, faceted (flat-shaded) silicon animals that glow from inner heat, sulfur yellows and ambers, waxy ammonia pastels and tar-dark methane reds.

**Builds and alien variants** (`vary` in `fauna.mjs`, on their own random stream so every other trait stays as it was). Every species has a build: ordinary; **giant** (megafauna at least 3 m and up to about 18 m, or 30 m for sky whales, in small, slow herds with relatively small heads and pillar legs; mostly grazers, rarely hunters; likelier in weak gravity); **heavy** (barrel bodies on short, thick legs; likelier in strong gravity); or **tall and thin** (stilt legs, long necks; likelier in weak gravity). About one species in three on complex worlds (more on exotic ones) is also stranger than its plan's usual forms: whole new forms (a **centipede** with 12–22 legs stepping in a wave, a walking **tower** crowned with feeding arms and a ring of eyes, a walking **dome** on thick legs with eyes on stalks, a **sky whale** with fins, flukes and gas sacs, a **siphon** chain of drifting bells) or alien features on a familiar body (a **hammerhead** bar with an eye at each end, eyes on tall **stalks**, **no head** at all with eyes across the chest above a vertical mouth, or a long **proboscis**). Builders get these too (except the drifting forms and the tube mouth), so intelligent species are as varied as the rest; walking towers and domes carry and wear things like any other body.

**Sea life** (`aquaticFor` in `fauna.mjs`, ids `aq-0`…). Worlds with open seas (water, methane, ammonia or sulfur, covering at least 5%) get swimmers, their own body plan: schools of **fish**, big **hunters** whose tall back fin cuts the surface, enormous filter-feeding **leviathans** (9–22 m) that come up to breathe, **squid** that swim mantle first, drifting **jellies**, **rays**, long undulating **ribbons** with frilled edges and spinning **discs** with eyes all round the rim. None of them is an Earth fish: each world's seas share one **body language** (`seaStyle`, stream 'sea-style'): two-, three- or five-fold symmetry, how many eyes and where (paired, on stalks, in a row, one great eye, or none but glowing pits), skin (smooth, plated, spined, frilled or see-through with glowing organs), mouths (jaws, a beard of feelers, a sucker, a sword-like rostrum, a glowing lure), how they swim (tail, fin rows, jets, whole-body ripples), fin shapes, segmented bodies and trailing filaments. Each species then takes its own body from that (`seaAnatomy`): body shape, back fins, tails (forked, fan, paddle, double, whip, jet funnel), rows of fins, arm count and kind for squid, extra wing pairs for rays, twin bells for jellies, and is named from it (lanternmaw, chainfin, spearnose, armcrown, fourwing, rimeye…), so two worlds' seas never look alike. Simple life gets only the small forms. Sea life is its own food web (nothing on land hunts it, nothing in the sea hunts on land), never drinks or stalks you,. In `fauna-mesh.js` each swimmer keeps to water deep enough for its size, picks a depth in its range every few seconds (deeper when fleeing or asleep), schools keep closer than herds, quick swimmers leap clear of the water now and then (more often when chasing or chased), and leviathans and some rays surface so their backs break the water. Fish swing their tails more than their heads, ribbons and leviathans flex up and down, discs spin. Sea life has its own instance budget (45% of the land's), so it never thins out the herds; species with no water near enough wait unseen until you reach a coast. Scan them from the shore (they can be scanned from farther than land animals).

**Civilized species look the part** (`fauna-culture.mjs`). A species only becomes a builder if it has something to build with, so every civilized species gets manipulators suited to its body plan, not human hands by default: jointed arms with fingered hands, a second smaller pair of arms on the chest, a prehensile trunk ending in fingers, a ring of fingered tendrils round the mouth, raised fingered forelimbs, clawed hands at the wing joints, or fingered tentacles hanging from a drifter. Visible culture then grows with the civilization level: body paint and a carried spear, staff or hammer (tool users); harness, sash, pouch, bead necklace, limb bands and plumes, crests or studs on the head (village builders); woven garments over the body, metal bands and a circlet (city builders); glowing seams and a wrist device (planet-shapers); a fitted suit, glowing collar ring, life-support pack, visor and a glowing rod (spacefarers). Colours, tools and ornaments come from each species' own seed, so no two cultures dress alike. Not every culture dresses, though: each has a style (`cultureOf` .style, on its own random stream). **Clothed** cultures follow the ladder above; **ornamented** ones wear no cloth, only beads, rings, studs and crowns (spacefarers a floating halo crown); **painted** ones paint their bare bodies in bold patterns that glow at higher levels; **bare** ones wear nothing and show their skill only in what they carry (a pouch, a metal vessel, tools; spacefarers a hovering device in a ring and a shimmering field round the head); **grown** ones wear living gear (sacs and pods on the back, grown crests, plates, glowing nodules, a membrane hood and a glowing gas sac in space) and are three times as likely to grow their homes (coral, living groves, wax combs, silk); **implants** cultures fuse metal plates and rings to their limbs and heads, with glowing ports and an eye band. Hands lean toward clothing; soft, radial and drifting bodies toward grown gear or nothing; armoured ones toward grown plates and implants; fliers toward ornament. Arms vary too (`armStyle`): jointed with fingers, boneless tentacle arms, pincers, or two pairs. Builders can have any build or alien feature (giant, heavy, tall and thin; hammerhead, eye stalks, headless; centipedes, walking towers and domes), and their buildings follow: giants build on a larger scale (unit up to 4.5), tall thin species taller, heavy ones lower.

Future systems should read the traits, not the derived values. Ecology (below) does.

`fauna-mesh.js` builds one model per species. The body, neck, head and tail are one smooth tube swept along a spine (tail, hips, belly or insect waist, shoulders, neck, skull, snout) with countershading and stripes, spots or a saddle on the back. Limbs are jointed tubes (hip, knee, ankle, toe) rooted inside the body, so everything connects. Eyes are eyeballs with pupils set into the head. Each species is drawn with instancing in one draw call, and animated in the vertex shader from a per-animal gait cycle: legs swing at the hip and fold at the knee on the forward swing, arms swing opposite the legs, wings flap, tails and tentacles curl toward the tip, coils ripple, and normals rotate with the limbs so lighting follows. A small behaviour model moves each animal over the sphere: grazers wander and graze in herds and bolt, hunters and curious species approach, fliers and drifters circle overhead; ground animals turn back at water, and everything stays within about 230 m of you. The animals' home area starts at the site and follows you across the world: herds left behind find fitting ground near you (never right beside you), and animals out of sight go there at once, so every part of a living world has its animals. Builders stay in their settlements. Get close enough to scan an animal and its species is recorded; observed species are saved per world and listed on the star map.

## Water

Seas are places to explore, not walls. **Wading:** anyone can walk into the shallows, up to 1.1 m deep (`WADE_DEPTH`), more slowly the deeper it gets; the camera follows the sea bed, not the surface, and you cannot jump in water over your waist. Lava and molten sulfur stay off limits. **The Petrel** (`makePetrel` in `kestrel.js`) is a new ship, not a repainted Kestrel: a rounded, keeled hull with a teal stripe, a bubble canopy, gull wings with floats, twin canted fins and three engines; once it is unlocked the HUD, toasts and prompts call your ship the Petrel. **Diving** (Pressure hull I, `diveDepth` in `upgrades.mjs`): the Petrel seals its hull, folds its wings back along the hull, puts out headlamps and a ducted propeller and trails bubbles (`kestrel.js`); it keeps 2.4 m clear of the sea floor, refuses water shallower than 3.5 m, and goes no deeper than its rating (40 m; Pressure hull II: any depth). Its scanner reaches twice as far as yours on foot. **Swimming** (Dive suit, `swimDepth`): past the shallows you swim instead of turning back; you move where you look, **Space** or **R** rise, **F** sinks, down to 30 m and never below the sea floor; back in the shallows you stand up and wade out. You can leave the Petrel under water and board it again there. Water seas from the start; methane and ammonia seas need Cryo seals; molten sulfur never. **Under water** (`updateUnderwater` in `game.js`) the view closes in to the sea's own colour, light fades with depth and at night, the surface is seen from below, rain stops, and the Petrel's lamp lights the water ahead.

**Sea plants** (`seaFloraFor` in `flora.mjs`, ids `sea-0`…): kelp forests (tall stems hung with blades and gas floats), seagrass meadows, branching coral (likelier in warm seas), lattice fans, anemone-like polyps, bladderweed strung with gas bladders, and on microbial worlds layered reef mounds. Each has a depth range and grows on the sea floor in the same scatter grid as land plants (`flora-mesh.js`), thickest in the sunlit shallows; they sway slowly with the swell and the wind does not reach them.

**Land, water and air overlap** (`habitatsFor` in `fauna.mjs`, `habitats: { land, water, air }`). Every ground animal wades into the shallows (about knee-deep for its legs). Some land animals **swim** as readily as they walk (coils, slugs and four-legged animals most often): they go out into the water for a swim, float with their backs at the surface, and hunt there. Some fliers **perch**: they land to walk and feed, and roost on the ground at night, their wings folded. Some hunting fliers **dive** into the sea after fish. Some swimmers **glide**: they leap from the sea and sail far over the water on wing-like fins. The food web follows: a hunter only takes prey it can reach (land, water, or both), so an amphibious hunter takes fish and a diving flier plucks them from the sea.

**Sea civilizations.** Sea life can become intelligent too (most easily the many-armed squid), though without fire under water the ladder is harder to climb. Sea builders build on the sea floor: settlements, towns and capitals are placed in open water at least 4.5 m deep, so no roof breaks the surface, and built on the sea bed (`seaFloorTerrain` in `settlements.mjs`, used by `networkFor` and `structures-mesh.js`), in three sea cultures (`structures-mesh.js`): **conch** builders live in true spiral shells, banded and knobbed, with a lit aperture; **polyp** builders grow colonies of tubes with tentacle fringes and glowing throats; **kelpweave** builders weave domes and arches of living kelp held up by floats. All are lit from inside; their lights glow up through the water at night. They spread across their world like any other civilization, and their colonies go only to worlds with seas.

**Documenting.** What lives only under water (sea plants, and swimmers that never leave the sea) is recorded like anything else, but counted apart: the HUD tally shows it as **Under water**, and fully documenting a world does not need it, since you can only reach it once you can dive (`seaOnly` and `totalsOf` in `codex.mjs`).

## Ecology

Animals now live off each other and their world (`ecology.mjs`, acted on in `fauna-mesh.js`). Everything comes from the traits above, drawn from its own key path so no existing species changed:

- **Day and night.** Each species is diurnal, nocturnal, crepuscular (dawn and dusk) or active at any hour. Dim stars, glowing markings, keen senses and a hunter's diet favour the night. Outside its hours an animal walks back to its herd and sleeps there, settled low on folded legs, and wakes when its time comes. Worlds that always show their star one face have no day cycle, so everything on them is active at any hour. Every world with days keeps at least one ground species awake in daylight, since that is when you usually land.
- **The food web.** Carnivores hunt ground animals smaller than themselves (fliers take only small prey, and other hunters only if much smaller, so no two species hunt each other). Omnivores eat plants and now and then take small non-hunters. Fliers and drifters are never prey. Descriptions name what a species hunts and what hunts it.
- **Hunting.** A hungry hunter that sees prey (perception sets the range) stalks at a walk, then sprints, and gives up when its stamina (metabolism, aggression) runs out or the prey outruns it. Fliers swoop down. Prey that spot a hunter bolt, and the alarm spreads through their herd. Big or aggressive grazers turn and face the hunter instead, and hunters soon give up on them. A caught animal disappears and comes back later at its herd, out of your sight. Pack-mates share a kill, and hunters eat big meals seldom. If a catch happens near you, a message tells you what you saw.
- **Eating and drinking.** Hunger and thirst build at a rate set by metabolism. Grazers walk to an actual plant and eat it with their heads lowered (where there are no plants they graze the ground cover). On worlds with seas, animals walk to the shore and drink. Trips are limited to what an animal can walk in about a minute and a half.
- Sleeping animals notice less: skittish ones let you come closer, and hunters find them easier prey.

## Intelligence

**Minds.** Every animal has a mind level, shown in its description when you observe it ("Mind: city builders (intelligence high, shapes things with tentacles)") and next to each observed species on the star map. Below culture it is instinctive, simple, cunning, or clever without culture. Above that comes the civilization ladder: **tool users, village builders, metalworkers, city builders, industrial, planet-shapers, orbital, spacefarers, system-shapers**. Climbing needs intelligence most, then cooperation (sociality), then a body that can shape things (`manipulatorsOf` in `fauna.mjs`): hands are best, then tentacles, raptorial forelegs, pincers and pedipalps, mandibles, beaks and feet, fins and coils, and rollers barely at all. Older biospheres gate the higher steps. In the galaxy as a whole, the most advanced species in a system reaches tool use in about 1 system in 17, villages in 1 in 39, cities in 1 in 96, planet-shaping in 1 in 211, spaceflight in about 1 in 600, and system-shaping (which also needs a biosphere over 8 billion years old) in about 1 in 2200. Cities are built by bipeds, radial walkers, drifters, spiders and insects.

**Architecture follows the body** (`structures.mjs`). Each body family has its own building cultures, picked per species from its traits and world, so two species of the same shape can build very differently. Only species with hands build houses, halls and rockets:

| Builders | Cultures |
|---|---|
| bipeds with hands | timber roundhouses, longhouses and tiered towers · flat-roofed pueblos and stepped pyramids (deserts) · hide tents, yurts and many-peaked pavilions (wandering kinds) · stilt houses on walkways (wet worlds) |
| octopus and star walkers | grown, coral-like branches, lumps and great fans · secreted spiral shells |
| three-legged walkers | pods raised high on three legs · ringed enclosures around carved pillars |
| gas-filled drifters | platforms tethered in the air under gas-bags · frond-stalks hung with bulb dwellings · masts hung with pods |
| six-legged | honeycomb towers of hexagonal wax cells · banded earth spires and tunnelled mounds |
| eight-legged | silk tents, canopies and funnel webs on poles · trapdoor burrows with lids and chimneys |
| coils, slugs, snails | domed burrows with ramps and glowing skylights, terraced amphitheatre pits · spiral shell towers |
| fliers | nest perches and perch-trees · rookery stacks full of ledges · woven nests hanging from arches |
| four-legged, handless bipeds | turf barrows inside earth banks and henges · domes of living trees woven together · domes |
| rollers | wheel-shaped houses and banked loop tracks · domes |

Grown cultures build from their own bodies' products (shell, silk, wax, living wood, membrane); the rest use the world's own materials: timber where forests grow, ice on frozen worlds, clay in deserts, glass on silicon worlds, otherwise stone. Accents use the species' own colours, buildings scale with the builders' size, and weak gravity builds taller.

**Temperament and extent** (`civilizations.mjs`). Tier says what a civilization *can* build; its temperament says how far it spreads and how it treats its land:

- **Ambition** (from aggression, metabolism and sociality, plus a culture roll) decides how far it expands.
- **Cohesion** (from sociality): a few great, crowded places, or many scattered ones.
- **Harmony** (from a plant diet, caution, and grown cultures): living lightly on the land, or clearing and paving it.

Together with the tier they give an **extent**: local, regional, continental, planetary, or **system**, where ambitious spacefarers settle the nearest rocky worlds and moons of their system (`colonistOf`). Colonists live under glass pressure domes around a spaceport, whatever they build at home. A colony's description and the star map name the world they came from, and the colonists themselves walk the colony. They wear pressure suits and helmets when the colony differs too much from home (`needsSuit`: air pressure outside half to double, temperature more than 35 K apart, a different biochemistry or a different state of water). Their home world has them natively. The temperament is part of the civilization's description.

**The settlement network** (`settlements.mjs`). Each civilization's world gets a fixed network, deterministic like everything else:

- The **capital** stands beside the landing site (on open, dry, fairly level ground, possibly a coast), so you land next to it.
- Depending on the extent, **cities, towns and villages** spread over a region, a continent or the whole planet, never overlapping.
- They are joined by **roads**: a spanning tree from the capital that avoids crossing much water.

The star map summarises it once you have recorded the settlement: "Settlements: a capital, 5 cities, 19 towns and 26 villages".

**What a place looks like** (`structures-mesh.js`). The capital and up to three more places near the site are built in full, grown rather than stamped:

- a centre (a gathering place or great structure on a plaza);
- 3–8 streets winding outward, and a ring road through big cities;
- buildings on plots along the streets, facing them, bigger and taller toward the core;
- landmarks in the core of cities, gaps for yards;
- street lamps for planet-shapers and above, along both sides of the streets with their arms over the road, in the builders' own way (a lantern hanging from a curled arm, a frosted orb on a post, or, for cultures that grow their homes or glow themselves, a glowing bulb on a curled stalk); each throws a pool of light on the ground and a soft halo, fading in as night falls; and crates for species with hands;
- fields beyond the edge (fenced by those with hands), lattice radio masts with blinking lights for planet-shapers and up, tracking dishes (a parabolic dish on a yoke, a receiver on struts at its focus) for spacefarers, and a spaceport beside the capital.

Streets, plazas and roads are strips and discs laid along the terrain, resampled about every 1.4 m along and in two or three lanes across, every vertex on the ground plus a lift, so the land never shows through them. They are a mesh of their own (capped at 48k vertices). Walkers have earth tracks before they build cities and paved roads after. Fliers, drifters, burrowers and hives need no roads, and tool users mark trails with standing stones. Other towns within 900 m get full dwellings too; farther ones are simple clusters of the culture's buildings. Cities and above light them at night, and so do colonies (`city-lights.js`): points on the night side of the world, seen from orbit and from the system view, fading out across the terminator. Size by tier:

- **Tool users**: camps with a hearth, shelters in their own style, and cairns.
- **Village builders**: a village around a gathering place, and others nearby if ambitious.
- **Metalworkers**: villages with stone forges, glowing furnaces and smoking chimneys.
- **City builders**: a city around a great structure (pagoda, stepped pyramid, pavilion, great comb, funnel web, giant spiral, amphitheatre, henge, perch-tree, great loop…), with towns and villages around it.
- **Industrial**: cities ringed by factories under saw-tooth roofs, banded smokestacks trailing smoke, and water towers; their first radio is heard out to 30 light years.
- **Planet-shapers**: a capital and cities across a continent or the whole world, lit at night, with radio heard from the next star.
- **Orbital**: all that, plus a launch site, tracking dishes, satellites and a station in orbit, but no crewed ships yet.
- **Spacefarers**: all that, plus a spaceport beside the capital built for their own ship design (`ships.js`), and a launch site in their own idiom: rockets on gantries (hands), grown launch towers (tentacled walkers), a giant tripod cradle, a tether climbing out of sight into orbit (drifters), launch silos (hives, spiders, burrowers), a rail up a towering perch (fliers) or a long launch ramp (grazers and rollers). Satellites fly in tidy constellations (a few orbital planes, evenly spaced, lower ones faster), each with a dish facing the world and solar wings that turn to the star (grown cultures fly pods with leaf-sails), and a spinning ring station with a docking port at each end. **Ships fly real routes**: shuttles lift off their pads, climb to the station, dock, and come back down; a liner hops from the capital to the nearest towns in turn and sets down beside each; freighters come in from deep space to the station's far port and leave again. Each flight is a loop of legs (`flights` in `structures-mesh.js`), so a ship's position is a pure function of time and can follow the moving station. Exhaust glows while climbing and fades at rest. **Colonies fly their home world's ships**, satellites and station, whatever they build on the ground.
- **System-shapers**: all that, busier, and the whole system made theirs (`megastructures.js`, drawn for every world so it is seen from the ground and from space): an orbital ring around the home world passing over the capital, joined to the ground by three elevators with climbers riding up and down; colonies on every rocky world and moon; habitats (spinning cylinders with lit window bands) around every giant; and four rings of mirrors circling the star. Ships of any civilization with colonies cross between its worlds as lights with short trails. Their home world is a **world-city**: every stretch of dry land away from the places built in detail is covered by instanced towers (mostly low blocks, tall where districts gather, parks between), lit by the star by day with windows lighting up across the night side, so the whole night side glows from orbit. Their capital is a megacity: an arcology at the centre and a skyline of towers in many forms (stepped, twisted, twin with a sky bridge, ringed, blade, needle, terraced gardens), each in its own glass and trim, with air traffic weaving above the rooftops.

**Paths** (`civilizations.mjs` `PATHS`). Tier says how far a civilization has come; its path says what it cares about, from its traits and temperament: **agrarian** (granaries, mills, more fields), **devotional** (a temple by the square, shrines where the streets run out), **scholarly** (an observatory, a hall of records), **mercantile** (market stalls round the square, warehouses), **martial** (a wall with gates and watchtowers) or **expansionist** (a founders' monument; the path that leads to system-shaping). Each path also adds its own skyscraper forms to megacities (farm towers, cathedral spires, observatory crowns, bastions, billboard blades). A path is saved as a name.

**Contact.** Each civilization has a disposition toward you (welcoming, wary, guarded, or curious for orbital civilizations and up), from its traits and leaned by its path, and something it could teach for its tier and for its path; the settlement card shows both.

**Collision.** The ship can't fly through buildings: it slides round towers and rides up over roofs it meets near the top. World-city towers, space elevators and orbital rings are solid too, on foot and in flight.

**Built for their world and their bodies.** Every building reads the world's conditions (`clime` in `structures-mesh.js`): snow settles on every upward face on cold worlds and chimneys smoke, a hot sun bleaches colours and brings wind towers and shade awnings, and damp darkens the foot of walls toward moss; strong gravity keeps towers low and weak gravity lets them soar. The institutions each path builds come in each builder's own idiom (`IDIOMS`): those who build with stone, timber, adobe, ice or glass raise columned temples and silos; those who grow their homes, swelling bulb shrines, storage pods, eye-like observatories and thorn hedges; hives, great waxen cells, comb stores and comb walls; spiders, soaring silk cathedrals, web dishes and web fences. Forges and factories follow too (stone forges or kiln mounds; smokestacks in brick, steel or stone, or clusters of grown, waxen or spun vats). Each family sets them on its own ground (`mounted`): fliers raise them on perches, drifters hang them under gas-bags, burrowers sink them into mounds, and fliers and drifters keep watch from towers instead of walls. System-shapers with hands raise their skylines in their culture's idiom (timber: pagodas of flared roofs; terraced: ziggurats with stairs and a shrine; tents: masts hung with tiers of fabric and guyed to the ground; stilts: towers striding on four legs with sky bridges; domed cultures: dome on dome), with our own glass forms only as an occasional extra, and crown their capital with that form writ large plus a crown of their own (a dome, halo rings, an open frame or a garden). Each species also lights its walls its own way (`lightStyle`): bands of light, vertical seams, round ports, a spiral, scattered glows, or, for a few, rows of windows much as we would; the world-city uses the same pattern in its shader, in the species' own light colour. Megacity towers and the world-city follow the same families (spires; roost towers ringed with landing ledges; towers floating on tethers under gas-bags; domes over deep shafts; grown spires; hexagonal shafts) in the world's own material and colours, with forms for hot worlds (louvred fins) and cold ones.

**Every building its own.** Each dwelling takes its walls, roof and trim from its culture's palette with a shift of its own, and about two in five have something of their owners' life beside them, by path: hay bales or a grain bin, a wayside shrine or prayer pole, a sundial or telescope, a market stall, a row of stakes and a banner, a mast with a pennant; spacefarers add solar panels.

**Ships** (`ships.js`). Each spacefaring civilization has its own ship design, chosen from 18 hull types by body family (and by culture for species with hands): needles, lifters, saucers, seeds, spirals, landers, gas bladders, combs, beetles, silk-sailed spindles, egg-sac clusters, drill-nosed bores, delta wings, feathered ships, rings, barrows and wheels. Length, girth, segments, fins, engines and colours (hull, the species' own colour as trim, glow) vary per civilization. The spaceport is a paved apron laid on its highest ground so no hill pokes through; the apron is flat but the world is round, so on small worlds the whole spaceport is built smaller until its rim stands at most 4 m above the curve (`PORT_DROP`), and its skirt reaches down to the ground, with marked pads and shuttles, a control tower, and the main ship either upright in a lattice launch tower or level beside an arched hangar. On worlds with no flat dry ground it is an offshore platform. Ships fly circuits over the capital and orbit the world, and the description names the hull ("they fly swept delta wings").

**Landing.** Terrain gives every world a `landing` point: the old pad position if that is dry and level, otherwise the nearest dry, level ground within 90 m of the site. On ocean worlds the site search reaches nearly the whole globe for land. "Find landing site" flies there, and herds never start in water.

**Detail.** Every culture's dwellings are built from many parts: stilt houses have braced piles, a railed deck, board walls and a layered thatched roof; shells have ridges, spines and a flared lip; hives have bevelled cells, wax caps and doors; balloon towns have railed gondolas under banded gas-bags held by guy lines; and so on for all 23 cultures. Around each dwelling is a **yard** (`yard()`): stepping stones to the door, pots and jars, tended plants, wood stacks and drying racks for species with hands, stones, and lamps for planet-shapers, each on the actual ground and never over water or across the door.

**Level ground.** Buildings with a solid floor are levelled: they stand on the highest ground under them (sampled at 17 points) on a flared foundation in the culture's own material that reaches down to the lowest, so no slope pokes through a floor. The terrace stops at 2 m on very steep ground. Launch sites and far-off houses are levelled the same way. Fields, paving slabs and hearths follow the ground instead: tilled soil is draped over it and every crop, fence post and stone stands on the ground where it is, with fence rails running post to post. Only dwellings may stand out in the shallows; fields always stay on dry land. Stilt, pole and floating buildings keep their legs in the ground instead.

On laptops the places near the site are capped at about 250k vertices and the whole world at 320k (plus up to 90k of streets); phones (`coarse`) keep the lighter 132k / 158k / 48k, so a spacefaring capital builds in about 0.2 s and draws in three calls.

Builders clear their ground, so most plants inside a settlement are gone. Everything is merged into three meshes (lit, glowing, and ground strips), whatever the size: three draw calls. Vertices are capped (far-off towns stop at the budget), so the biggest world builds in under 0.1 s. Footings reach into the ground, so nothing floats on a slope. Buildings are solid on foot, while fields, roads and hearths can be walked over. Walking within about 45 m of a settlement, or flying low over it, records it: a message describes the builders, and the star map lists the world with their tier. Builders also clear rocks and most plants from their ground.

**Radio signals** (`signals.mjs`). Planet-shapers and spacefarers broadcast, and their signals carry much farther than the jump drive: planet-shapers are heard out to 80 light years, spacefarers out to 150, once you have the Long-range receiver (the Kestrel's own receiver hears 30% of that: 24 and 45). Every source in the full range is found and cached; the map filters by what you can hear, and labels them "unidentified" until the Signal decoder is installed. Every system within 150 light years of your star is checked in a background thread (`signals-worker.js`, or in small slices if workers are unavailable), taking well under a second on a laptop, and the result is cached per star. On the map, each source is a gold signal mark at every zoom, out to the galaxy view, and you can tap it. The side panel lists them nearest first (**RADIO SIGNALS · N HEARD**). Selecting one says who lives there, how far it is, and that you can set a course and fly there, or jump closer star by star. Within jump range they also get a gold ring and a SIGNALS tag. Ocean worlds with no dry land near the landing site have nowhere to build.

**Life on the map.** In the system view, surveyed worlds with life carry the same rings as stars on the galaxy chart (dashed for microbes, bolder for animals, violet for exotic chemistry), and the world list tags them FAINT LIFE, LIFE or RICH LIFE, plus EXOTIC. A selected world says how rare its life is galaxy-wide, for example "Complex life with animals: very rare, about 1 world in 76". The odds are listed separately for water-based and exotic life, and `starmap.test.mjs` checks them against the generator.

**Danger without combat.** Some big, aggressive hunters (`stalksYou` in `ecology.mjs`; never intelligent species) see an explorer on foot as prey. About 1 world in 8 with animals has one, and their descriptions say so. A stalker that notices you (within its sight range, while it is awake) closes in at a walk, then charges and lunges. Fliers swoop. After a lunge it backs off for 10–18 s. Each lunge knocks you back and takes 16–45% of your suit's integrity. The suit recovers once you have been left alone for 6 s, and a readout shows it while it is damaged. If it fails, the Kestrel's emergency recall puts you back beside the ship. Aboard the ship you are always safe. There are no weapons: the answer is to keep moving, get back aboard, or come back by day if the hunter is nocturnal.

The star map's **long-range scan** senses biosignatures at stars within jump range before you visit: green rings on the chart (dashed for faint), and the panel and jump list say whether life is faint (microbial), clear (plants) or strong (complex life with animals).

## Skies

Worlds with air have a sky (about 0.03 bar and up; thicker air gives a stronger, brighter sky). Its colour is the world's atmosphere tint, lightly tinted by its star: bright by day, shifting warm at sunrise and sunset, and dark at night, from where the star sits in your sky. It fades back to black as you climb toward orbit, the stars fade out in daylight, and haze softens distant land. Airless worlds keep the black sky of space. Seas are lit as smooth spheres, not with the ground's facets.

**Clouds** (`clouds.mjs`, drawn by `cloud-mesh.js`). Every world with enough air (about 0.04 bar and up) has a cloud layer 55–130 m above sea level (lower on small worlds) to fly through. Seen from space the layer sinks to just above the highest ground as you climb away, so it hugs the world as real clouds do; extra shells a little higher hold only the thicker cloud (two on phones), and clouds are shaded by whether they rise toward the star, so they have tops and sides. What it is made of follows the world: white water cloud over seas and wet land, wispy ice cloud on cold worlds, a near-total sulfur deck on toxic worlds, ash over volcanic ones, orange organic haze on methane worlds, and thin dust on dry ones. Wetter worlds and thicker air give more cover; thin air gives less, and fainter, cloud. One shell per world is both the cloud cover seen from orbit and the overcast seen from the ground, so they always agree. Clouds are lit by the star (warm at the terminator; on the night side faintly lit like the ground, and from below at night just lighter than the sky), fade out when their world is a small, distant disc, thin toward the world's edge, are grey underneath where they are thick, and cast soft shadows on the ground. They drift a few metres a second, stretched east–west in gentle latitude bands, and the fine detail drifts faster than the big systems, so clouds change shape as they move. The pattern is the same noise in JS and in the shader (a hash exact in 32-bit floats) and is driven by the orbit clock, so everyone sees the same clouds at the same moment, and flying into one (where there is cloud, not in the gaps) closes the view to a few dozen metres of cloud-coloured fog. Phones use fewer noise octaves.

**Weather** (`weather.mjs`, drawn by `weather-fx.js`). What falls from the clouds follows them: rain from water cloud (snow when the world is below freezing), snow from ice cloud, acid drizzle from sulfur cloud, ash from volcanic ash cloud, slow fat drops of methane rain from organic haze, and blowing dust on dry worlds (lifted by strong wind, not dropped by cloud). It falls where the cloud overhead is thick, so you can see a shower coming and fly out from under it, and only below the cloud base. Storms come and go in 15-minute windows, like the aurorae: each window may bring a storm or two, each a patch of the world a couple of hundred metres across that drifts with the clouds and piles them into a thick mass (bright from above, dark from below) with heavy rain or snow and strong wind. Water, sulfur and ash storms have lightning, which lights the clouds from inside. Under heavy cloud the day dims and the sky turns grey; falling rain, snow, ash and dust close in the view. The wind (`wind.mjs`) blows along the cloud drift and swings round in storms; plants and grass lean and gust with it. A message says when it starts to rain (or snow...) where you are, or a storm arrives. Like the clouds, everything comes from the seed and the orbit clock, so everyone sees the same weather at the same moment. What falls is a box of particles that travels with the camera, animated in the vertex shader: one draw call, fewer particles on phones.

## Rendering and travel

Every planet and moon, including the one you are on, is drawn by one level-of-detail system (`planet-lod.js`). Each world is a cube projected onto a sphere, and each face is a quadtree of tiles. A tile splits into four finer tiles when the camera is closer than about twice its width, and merges back as you leave. Detail therefore rises smoothly as you approach anything, so a nearby moon sharpens too and there is no jump when entering orbit. Close up, triangles are about 0.6 m across. Changes between detail levels are smoothed (geomorphing): every vertex also stores where it sits in the next coarser level, and the shader blends toward that by distance, so a tile already looks exactly like its parent when the two are swapped, and tile edges meet coarser neighbours without gaps. Skirts are lit with the ground's own normals, so any remaining hairline gap is invisible. Tiles beyond the horizon are not drawn, and the coarser tile stays visible until its four children are ready, so detail never leaves holes. Skirts along tile edges, on land and water, hide seams between tiles of different detail. Tiles are built in background threads (`terrain-worker.js`, falling back to the main thread if workers are unavailable), and the coarsest level of every world is built at load so nothing pops in empty. Water and lava are tiles too, matching the terrain's detail. A logarithmic depth buffer keeps distant seas and coastlines from flickering.

Up close the ground gets extra detail from its shader, at no cost in triangles: grain and speckle on grass and sand, a network of cracks on rock, ice and crust, pits in volcanic and toxic crust, and a slight bump so the light catches it. Each vertex carries what the ground is made of there (grass, bare rock, snow, from `terrain.mjs`), which picks the pattern. Every noise layer fades out once it gets smaller than a pixel, and the whole effect fades out between 14 m and 34 m, so distant ground never shimmers. Phones get a lighter version without the cracks and finest layer.

Worlds keep realistic size ratios and are big against the ship (`scale.mjs`). One Earth radius is 580 m × SIZE, and SIZE is 4 by default: small rocky planets are about 1 km in radius, Earth-sized ones about 2.3 km and the biggest super-Earths about 4 km (`worldRadius`, `system.mjs`); moons are about 0.45–1.1 km (`moonRadius`). Giants are their real size in Earth radii (ice giants about 7–9 km, gas giants about 26 km, more when starlight puffs them up), and stars are 40 Earth radii per square root of their real radius (red dwarfs about 37 km, a Sun-like star about 93 km, A stars up to about 125 km; a real Sun would be 109 Earth radii and swallow the inner orbits). Orbits are still compressed but every gap grows with the worlds: the first orbit is about three star radii out, planets are at least 24 km apart, moons orbit half a planet radius plus 1.7 km above the surface, and systems span roughly 200–700 km. Try `?size=1` (the old compact scale) up to `?size=20` in the URL to compare; the choice is remembered until another `?size=` is given. Out in open space the engines scale with the distance to the nearest surface (1× within 2.5 km, up to 60×), so crossing a system by hand takes about a minute; cruise always takes 12 seconds and climbs over the star when the straight route would cross it. Orbits keep slow speeds along the track (inner planets about 30 m/s, moons about 45–55 m/s), so a ship can always catch a world. Site features (the landing pad, rock and plant regions) are measured in metres, so they look the same on any size of world; continents, seas, plant biomes and weather systems grow with the world. Cruising to a rocky world ends above its landing site. World coordinates are rebased on arrival. Cruise moves through space above the orbital plane and can be disengaged; manual flight crosses between worlds too.

On foot you collide with rocks, plant trunks and bushes, and the parked ship (grasses and microbial mats are walked through), sliding along them rather than stopping dead. Space jumps; jump height follows the world's gravity, so low-gravity moons give long, floaty leaps over low obstacles. Click the view to capture the mouse: moving it then looks around, on foot and in flight, without holding a button (Esc frees the cursor). On touch devices a joystick moves you, dragging the view looks around, and the HUD collapses to the essentials.

Giants (`giant-mesh.js`) are a smooth sphere with a generated cloud map (bands bent by turbulence, streaks, sometimes a storm), a haze at the limb and, if ringed, rings with gaps. The map is sampled on the sphere, so it has no seam, and the planet turns slowly. Giants are several to a dozen Earths across (see above), always far bigger than any rocky world, and get extra orbital room so their moons stay clear of neighbours. They have no surface: cruising to one stops in space on the day side, and you cannot fly closer than 250 m to the cloud tops (2% of the radius on the biggest). Their moons are landed on as usual.

## Look and performance

**Light and air** (`atmosphere.js`). One smooth lighting model for everything (the stepped cel bands are gone): light wraps a little past the terminator, and the star casts soft shadows in a box about 140 m across (84 m on phones) that follows you near the ground, moved in whole shadow-map texels so shadows don't crawl, and switched off above about 260 m and at night. Plants near you cast them (swaying with the plant, cut out by the leaves), as do rocks, formations, buildings and the ship; everything on the ground receives them (animals too: their shadows are drawn in the same pose, so the shadow's legs walk with them). `?shadows=0` turns them off. The sky is a dome: a deeper zenith, a paler hazy horizon (paler in thicker air), the ground's colour below and a glow round the star; distant land fades into the horizon's colour, and clouds, storms, the local haze and water take the sky over. The light takes each world's mood: the star warms at dusk, the sky fill light is the air's own colour, light bounced from below is the ground's, nights are darker, and dim stars and thick air lower the exposure a little.

**One surface style** (`soften.js`). Rocks, formations, trunks, logs and solid plant parts keep their low-poly facets, but the light runs smoothly across them: each normal is a blend of its face's own and the smooth one round that corner (corners sharper than 70°, like a cylinder's rim, stay crisp; crystals, glass, ice blades and basalt columns stay fully faceted). Leafy plants are shaded to match: leaves deep inside and under a crown are darker, and leaves seen against the sun glow with their own colour (`LEAF_TRANSLUCENT`, look.js). No crown is a solid shell any more: fir skirts and boughs, vase-shaped crowns, brushes and every other piece of tree foliage are leaf cards over the shape's own surface (flora-leaves.js `leafSurface`), the vase crown is built of leafy clumps, and distant trees use rounded masses instead of cones. A crown of very many small clumps gets fewer leaves on each, so a tree stays within about a thousand cards.

**Restraint in colour** (`palette.mjs`). Each world's leaves are drawn toward its colour key (40% of the way for the main leaf colour, 30% for the second), with their saturation kept under the world's ceiling; bark keeps its hue and only has its saturation capped. The sky's horizon haze takes a little of the land's own colour at the horizon's brightness, and the zenith is a touch less saturated, so ground, plants and sky read as one small palette per world.

**The smaller plants** (`flora-understory.js`, `understory-plans.mjs`). Shrubs, succulents, fungi, stalks, bulbs, sheets, filaments, chimneys and plumes each grow to one of several plans, picked per species, so they vary as much as the trees: shrubs as a fountain of arching stems, flat tiers, a thicket of switches, a low cushion or a broom; succulents as rosettes of thick pointed leaves, ribbed barrels, chains of paddles, pebbles or clumps of tubes; fungi as crowded clusters, shelves on old stumps, coral, puffballs or honeycombed morels; stalks as reed beds, giant flowers, horsetails or bamboo; bulbs as pitchers, floating balloons, stacked gourds or hanging lanterns; sheets as frills or fans; filaments as arches or tassels; chimneys as clusters of pipes or stacked rings; plumes as feathery fronds or pompoms. The original model is one plan among them. Leafy clumps become leaf cards, and everything solid has softened facets. The plan adds a phrase to the species' description.

**Under water too.** Kelp grows as ribbons, giant stipes with a canopy or low bushes; sea grass as eelgrass meadows, paired paddles or spiky tufts; coral as brain domes, tables, pillars, staghorn antlers or stacked cups; fans as wavy plates or bare whips; anemones as tall tubes, wide carpets or crowds; bladders as grape bunches or arching chains; reef mounds as layered columns or terraces (the original models stay as one plan among them).

**Plant surfaces.** The solid parts of plants have a texture drawn in the shader, from the plant's rest pose so it stays put as it sways: bark is furrowed up the stem, broken into plates with fine grain and a little lichen on what faces up; caps, bulbs and fleshy leaves are softly mottled with darker flecks. Logs, stumps and boulders are mottled too.

**Distance.** The lighter models of far plants keep every trunk and limb piece (only twigs go), leave fruit and blossom out, and are leafy like the near ones; leaves far off are drawn denser so they do not thin away. Big species never get the small-plant plans (pebbles, puffballs, barrels, gourds, balloons, cushions).

**No popping.** Plants switch between their near, far and distant models over a band of distance where both are drawn, each covering the share of pixels the other leaves (a screen-door dissolve), and they fade in at the edge of view the same way instead of growing. Where the triangle budget runs out, plants fade out a little before that distance rather than stopping dead. Near models now reach 85 m (trees 60 m) and distant ones start at 200 m. To pay for it, plants under 1.2 m cast no shadows and the shadow map is redrawn every other frame.

**Stars.** Sunspots are small and few, in the active belts either side of the equator, a dark core in a paler penumbra (they were huge dark blotches).

**Clouds.** Cloud systems are a few hundred metres across with soft edges. From below, a bank is bright and thin at its edges, blue-grey and lumpy where thick, glows at the edges with the star behind it, and the higher shells show through its gaps for depth. Thin wisps drawn out along the wind hang below the deck, seen from the ground and low flight. From orbit the shells fade where seen edge-on, so the world has no ragged rim.

**Landing.** The ground under the ship may be up to about 24 degrees steep, measured 6 m either side along and across it.

**Water** (`water.js`). The sea's colour follows its depth (terrain tiles carry the depth of water over each point): pale and clear over the shallows, so the bed and the sea grass show through, dark far out. Foam swells and ebbs along every shore, small travelling waves move over the surface (fading with distance so far water stays calm), and at a glancing angle the water reflects the sky's colour. Lava keeps its own look.

**Forest mood** (`atmosphere.js`). Valley mist: low ground seen from above (valleys, lake basins, the woods below a ridge) fills with pale haze, more the lower and farther it is, strongest on wet worlds at dawn and dusk; the hill you stand on stays clear. It lives in the fog shader (a THREE.Fog whose near holds the distance fog's density and whose far holds the mist's strength). Sunbeams: in woods by day, pale shafts slant down through the canopy around you, brightest looking toward the star and flickering a little, fixed to the ground as you walk; none under heavy cloud. `?mist=0` and `?beams=0` turn them off.

`look.js` holds the game's look in one place. `applyLook(THREE)` runs before anything is drawn and replaces three.js's physically based lighting, which every `MeshStandardMaterial` uses (terrain, plants, animals, buildings, the ship), with a stylised model: light falls off in soft bands with a little of the smooth falloff kept underneath, a thin rim catches the sun on silhouettes, smooth surfaces such as water and glass get one crisp highlight, and the reflected-environment pass is skipped. After tone mapping every material is graded the same way (slightly more saturated, shadows leaning cool). The look is a handful of numbers in `STYLE`, so changing the whole game's mood is a one-line edit, and it is cheaper than the physical model. New materials pick it up automatically as long as they are `MeshStandardMaterial`.

Plants use two models per variant: the full one up close and, beyond 70 m (45 m on phones), a lighter one built from the same random numbers, with parts under 6% of the plant's height dropped and spheres, cylinders and cones rebuilt with fewer facets (`simplerPart`). On average the far model has half the triangles; trees have about a fifth. Plants are the biggest share of triangles on a living world, so this is what lets forests get denser.

Each plant model is one draw call: stems and foliage share one material, except on glowing species, whose foliage needs its own. Models with no plants in view are hidden, since an empty instanced mesh still costs a draw call.

Rendering resolution adapts to the frame rate: after two slow checks in a row (below 48 fps, checked twice a second) it steps down, to 55% at most, and after two fast ones (above 58 fps) it climbs back, at most once every two seconds. Changing resolution resizes the canvas, which wipes it, so the change is applied just before a frame is drawn; applying it after one showed a black frame.

**Colour key** (`palette.mjs`): each world has a few hues its life shares, anchored on its own ground and vegetation colour and arranged by one harmony scheme picked per world (analogous, complementary, split-complementary or triadic), plus a saturation ceiling, so some worlds are muted and some bold. Plant flowers, fruit and accents, coral, and animal colours are pulled most of the way toward the nearest hue of the key and kept under the ceiling (warning colours less so, camouflage not at all, and leaves keep their starlight-tinted green). Buildings take their paint from their builders' colours, so they follow too. The pull is applied after the colours are rolled, so no random numbers changed and every species is otherwise the same as before. Life on other chemistry keeps its own palettes.

Test switches, each remembered in the browser until switched back:

- `?look=classic` the original physical lighting; `?look=new` the stylised look (default)
- `?perf` an overlay with frame rate, worst frame, draw calls, triangles and resolution; `?perf=0` hides it
- `?stress=3` plants three times as dense, with three times the plant budget (1 to 8), to see how dense forests would run; `?stress=1` back to normal
- `?adaptive=0` fixed resolution; `?adaptive=1` adaptive again
- `?land=classic` the original gentler terrain and ocean coverage; `?land=new` the new landforms (default) (`style.mjs`)
- `?plants=classic` the original plant models, spacing, heights and ground cover, and no local air; `?plants=new` the new forests (default)

## Moving orbits

Every planet and moon moves along a circular orbit (`orbits.mjs`). Time is the real clock, so positions match between visits, survive a reload, and the star map shows where everything is now. Planets follow Kepler's third law: the innermost slot takes 10 minutes per orbit and periods grow with distance to the power 1.5, so outer worlds take hours. Moons of a rocky planet share one 3-minute period, so neighbouring moons never pass through each other; giants' moons orbit outside the rings and follow Kepler's law from an 8-minute innermost period. Nothing moves faster than about 50 m/s, well below cruising speed.

Coordinates in the scene are relative to the world you are at. Near a world (flying, landed, on foot) you travel with it, so nothing local changes; the star, sunlight and the other worlds move around you, and the day side turns as a world circles its star. In free space and on cruise the ship holds its course through the system, and cruises follow their destination along its orbit.

Distances and planet sizes are intentionally compressed. There is no combat, mining, inventory or crafting, by design: see `PROGRESSION.md` for the game's direction (knowledge and contact as the currencies).

## Scanning

On foot, aim the crosshair at a plant, an animal or a rock. When it is within reach, a ring appears around the crosshair, with the thing's name below it, or "Unknown animal" if you have not recorded one. **Hold E** (or the *Hold to scan* button on touch) until the ring fills, about 1.3 s. Look away, walk off or let go and it drains. A card then shows the name, a one-line description, labelled facts and tags, and what the scan counted toward. Aim at something you have already recorded and press **E** to see its card again. Under the world's name, the HUD has one row each for plants, animals and geology, showing how many are recorded. Messages appear at the top of the screen, clear of the crosshair and the ship marker. `scanning.js` finds what you are aiming at and builds the card; `scan-ui.js` draws the prompt and the card.

**Watching animals.** Once you have scanned a species, aim at one of them while it is doing something and the prompt changes to, for example, "Ridgeback · drinking" with *Hold E to observe*. Hold for 2 s while it keeps at it (watching works from about 1.6 times scanning range) and the behaviour is recorded: grazing, drinking, asleep, hunting, feeding on a catch, or standing its ground against a hunter. Which ones a species shows follows from its ecology (`behavioursOf` in `ecology.mjs`), so a night animal is only seen asleep by day and only hunters can be seen hunting. An animal's card lists what you have watched it do and what is left. Behaviours are codex entries of kind `behaviour`.

**Sky events** (`phenomena.mjs`, drawn by `sky-events.js`). Things that happen at a time, not a place:
- **Eclipses**: a moon crosses the star, or, from a moon, its planet does. Worked out from the real orbits, so the eclipse you see is the one in the 3D view. The daylight dims; in a total eclipse the stars come out, day animals settle down and night animals wake. Common on moons, rare from planets with small moons.
- **Aurorae**: curtains of light at night on worlds with air (0.05 bar and up), more often around flaring stars and on moons of giants. Colours vary by world.
- **Meteor showers**: at night on about half the worlds with air, the ones that cross a comet's trail.
Aurorae and meteor showers come in storms: time is cut into windows and each window's storm is drawn from its own key path, so everyone sees the same sky at the same moment. On a world, the last line of the HUD tally is a forecast for where you are ("Total eclipse in 3 min", "Aurora now · look up"). A message says when one begins. On foot, look at the star (eclipse) or up at the sky and hold E to record it (kind `phenomenon`, one per world and type).

## Stations

Orbital, spacefaring and system-shaping civilizations keep a station in orbit with a **landing deck** on top (`station-deck.js`, shapes in `station-shape.mjs`). Every civilization's is its own:

- **Plan**, from the builders' body family and varied by the seed: one round deck, a hexagon or octagon, a walkable ring on spokes round a hub, arms out from a hub with a pad at each end, a long spine with platforms along it, platforms linked by bridges, a honeycomb of hexagonal cells (hive builders), overlapping lobes (grown cultures) or spokes and rings like a web (web spinners). A deck is a union of discs, polygons and long rectangles; its rim is worked out from that, so every plan gets a parapet, and the crew find their way through the doorways between its parts.
- **Size**: at least 38 m in radius, larger by tier and more for ambitious civilizations, up to about 150 m. Bigger stations orbit higher and more slowly.
- **On the deck**: 3 to 6 pads (pale blue for visitors), a control spire at the hub in their style, their path's landmark facing their world (an observatory, a market, a shrine, greenhouses, watchtowers or a module under a crane), with more copies on bigger decks, plus hangars, tanks, gardens, kiosks, dishes, cargo by the crew's pads, benches and lamps along the rim.
- **Underneath**: a keel, a core down to the far port where freighters dock, a habitat ring spinning round it, and solar wings (leaf-sails for grown cultures). Shuttles from the spaceport set down on the crew's pads.

**Docking.** Fly within about 90 m of the rim, above the deck, below 80 m/s, and press **L** (the button and waypoint say when you can). The ship settles onto the visitors' pad. **E** steps out onto the deck, where gravity is a steady 1 g. **E** beside your ship boards it again, and **L** undocks. Decks and arks are solid, so you can't fly through them.

**The crew** are the builders in pressure suits, and they are busy. Each picks a job (servicing a pad, tending a garden, keeping a kiosk, studying at the observatory, repairing at a hangar door, standing a post at the spire, resting on a bench, looking out over the rail, talking with another), walks there round whatever is in the way, and works at it with their hands (an arm motion in the creature shader; species without hands bow to the work). Some carry crates from the cargo to the pads. When your ship is on its pad, someone comes to look it over. How they take you comes from their traits and path (`stanceOf`):

| Stance | Comes from | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| welcome | sociable (traders lean this way) | two leave their work to meet you and stay near you |
| curious | sharp senses (scholars, expansionists) | they stop to look; one circles you and scans you |
| wary | cautious (devotional, agrarian) | they keep their distance, watch you and back away if you come close |
| guarded | aggressive (martial, expansionist) | two wardens follow a few metres behind you wherever you go |

The first time you step onto a deck it goes into the codex as a **station** entry, with a card.

### Arks

Civilizations that have spread beyond their world (every system-shaper, and spacefarers who settle their system) also keep an **ark** (`civ.ark`, `civilizations.mjs`): many of them live aboard it. A few spacefarers (about one in ten) have gone further and left their world altogether (`civ.spaceborne`): their towns still stand, weathered, overgrown and dark at night: no one lives there, plants grow back through them, and nothing flies in their sky. An **ark** (`megastation.js`), one to two and a half kilometres across, parked sixty degrees ahead of their world along its orbit (its leading Lagrange point, `arkPosition`). Its form follows the builders: two turning habitat drums on a spine, a great wheel on spokes, a needle of stacked turning discs under a crown, a sphere with a lit equator and farm rings, or a cluster of grown pods. It is lit along its length and ringed with running lights, with a steady point of light that shows from anywhere in the system, it is marked (a gold diamond with its name) on the system map, and the waypoint points to it when you have no other target. Its landing deck sits on the part that doesn't turn: a mega deck up to 300 m across with up to 8 pads, several landmarks and a crew of 30.

## Codex overview and rarity

Press **K** (or the **CODEX** button) for everything you have recorded (`codex-ui.js`): a gallery with a picture of each thing, its name, where it was found and how rare it is. Plants, animals (and their behaviours), rocks, landforms and core samples are drawn with their own models, the same builders the worlds use, rendered once in a small offscreen renderer and kept for the session (`thumbs.js`); stations show their builders' ship, settlements and contacts their species, and worlds, sky events and signals are painted. Filter by kind or by rarity, sort by newest, rarest or name, and tap a record for a larger picture, its description and facts. Each record is rebuilt from its seed, so only the codex entries are saved.

**Rarity** (`rarity.mjs`) is worked out from the traits in each codex entry, so it is the same wherever it is read. Five grades, calibrated against the galaxy: common (about 65% of what there is to find), uncommon (21%), rare (11%), exceptional (2%) and legendary (1%). Rare things include glowing and strange plants and animals, life on other chemistries (silicon most of all), drifters and rollers, clever minds, landforms, eclipses, hunters at their kill and advanced civilizations; legendary ones are the rarest rocks (geodes, iron meteorites) and total eclipses. A scan card shows the grade as a tag, and finding something rare says so. Rarity is used:

- in **trade**: rarer things are worth more to other civilizations (`contact.mjs`);
- on the **upgrade line**: Jump drive III asks for 2 rare finds (tasks can ask for `rarity: { atLeast: 'rare' }`).

## Contact

Meeting another mind (`contact.mjs`, `contact-ui.js`). Press **E** beside one of an intelligent species: one of the crew on a station deck or ark, one of a household in their home, or (once you have scanned their species) one of them in their town or colony. A panel opens with who they are, how they take you, where you stand on the ladder (observed, understood, contact, trust, friendship) and how far their trust has grown. It never covers the one you are talking to: it is not modal (the world goes on; only you stand still), it sits at the side (a sheet along the bottom on narrow screens), the view turns to face them and the picture is shifted so they stand in the clear part of the screen. They turn to you and attend; they answer a greeting with their own gesture, are delighted (a gesture again) by a gift that matters to them, and speak (with sound on).

- **Who you can talk to.** Orbital civilizations and above meet you halfway and translate. Tool users to metalworkers need **Xenolinguistics**; city builders to planet-shapers need **Diplomatic protocols**. Without it, E says which upgrade would let you try.
- **Greeting.** The first meeting starts with a greeting. The wary step back unless you have been near them a while (20 s on their deck or on foot); the welcoming warm to you at once.
- **Trading knowledge.** They show what they hope to learn, and you choose from a few things in your codex. What they value follows their path: agrarians want plants, scholars sky events, rocks and strange chemistry, traders word of other peoples and stations, the devout the sky and old signals, the martial the dangers of other worlds, expansionists other worlds. Anything from another star is worth more; things from their own world, or given before, little or nothing. The curious prize the first thing you show them; the guarded take longer to trust. They take three things per meeting; a new meeting starts after half an hour.
- **What it brings.** Each step reached goes into the codex (`contact` entries) and moves the upgrade line: trust counts toward Diplomatic protocols, friendship with a spacefaring civilization toward the Albatross. At **trust** they share what they know: tool users to metalworkers show you where everything on their world lives (an arrow on foot, as Life sonar does, on their world); city builders and up give star charts (every system within 15 to 60 ly shows its worlds on the map). **Friendship** needs a second meeting; they give you a keepsake, and spacefarers their drive technology (+3 ly of jump range, from up to two friends). Relationships are saved per civilization (`elsewhere-relations-v1`).

## Intelligent species at home

Intelligent species in their own settlements live as **townsfolk** (`fauna-mesh.js` `setTown`, places from `surface.js`): each goes from one place in town to another (the doorstep of a building to work at, the edge of a square to talk in, a tall building to keep watch from, a stretch of street to stroll), walks round the buildings on the way and never through them, keeps to the streets on longer trips (where their builders lay streets: not every people does), works with its hands when it gets there (or bends to the work), goes indoors now and then, and mostly sleeps indoors. Builders are many more than a herd: a village has its households, a city its crowds. Hunters leave towns alone. Colonists do the same in their colonies.

- **Expression** (`express.mjs`, shared by townsfolk, wild animals and station crews). Heads turn (a head-and-neck part in the creature shader) to look at you, at whoever they are talking to, or at what they hunt; otherwise they glance about and shift their weight. Each species greets in its own way, from its body and culture: a bow, arms raised, a slow spin, a hop, a sway, rearing up, a crest flare, and glowing markings pulse as they do. Their stance toward you shows in how they stand: the curious lean in and tilt their heads, the wary shrink back, the guarded stand tall, the welcoming bounce.
- **Voices** (`voices.js`): each species has a voice of its own, synthesized from its body and nature (big ones low, fliers chirping, coils hissing, drifters humming in slides, rollers clicking; silicon life buzzes, glowing species shimmer), shaped by mood. Townsfolk near you talk among themselves now and then. Only with sound on.
- **Arrival.** Set down beside a town and they react as their stance has it: welcoming ones come out in a crowd to the edge of the landing ground and greet you; curious ones, a few, come close to look; wary ones go to their doorways and watch; guarded ones send two out to stand a few metres off.
- **Doing things together** (vignettes): every so often a few leave what they are doing for something their people do, by their path and the time of day: a market in the square (traders), a procession along the streets to the heart of the capital, bowing there (the devout), going out at night to watch the sky (scholars), changing the watch in pairs (the martial), working in the fields (farmers), building at the edge of town (expansionists), and gathering round the hearths at dusk. A line tells you when one starts near you.
- **The young**: a quarter of them, small, at play round a grown-up (chasing, hopping, spinning); they come to stare at you, unless their people are wary of you (then they hide behind a grown-up).
- **Kept animals**: builders from villages up keep a small grazing species from their world in pens round the edge of town. Otherwise **wild animals stay out of cities and big towns** (`keepOut`: herds that would live there move out; none wander in); villages and camps they still wander through.
- **City worlds** (`urban.mjs`, `city-plan.js`): the most advanced civilizations with the temperament for it (ambitious, close-knit, not ones who live lightly on their land or whose life turns on the harvest) build over most of their home world. Inside the city the land is levelled into terraces (terrain.mjs), coloured as paving and roofs from orbit with parks left green, lit all over its night side, and empty of wild plants and animals outside the parks. Around the capital (all towers) a planned grid of blocks runs to the horizon: each on a flat podium, with towers in their builders' idiom (set-back boxes, hex prisms, shells and domes, needle spires, stepped terraces, stacked rings, spun lattices), tallest at the heart and lower toward the edges, windows drawn in the shader and lit at night, streets between the blocks that the townsfolk walk. Skylines are lower on small worlds. Spacefarers and up who don't pave their world still live in modern towers, not huts.
- **Homes** (`interior.js`, `interior-look.js`): press **E** at a house's door to go in. The room is built only then (and thrown away when you leave), at the size of the house: houses are built to their builders' size, so a giant's hall is vast, and some species' homes are too low for you to enter (you look in at the door instead). What is inside follows how they live: a hearth, work table, shelves and mat for hand-builders; perches and a nest-bowl for nest-builders; earthen alcoves and a moss bed for burrowers; glowing wax cells for the hive; silk hammocks and threads for web-builders; troughs and soft mounds for grazers; resting bowls and ramps for rollers; pools for tentacled builders; lean-boards for tripods (they don't sit). Walls, floors and ceilings are painted by what the house is built of (boards, stone blocks, plaster, weave, wax cells, packed earth, shell; modern panels and tiles for those who reach space), with a rug and hangings in the household's own colours and pattern, shelves and tables cluttered with pots, bowls, tablets, plants and crystals, windows letting in daylight, and a lamp. Advanced homes glow (lit panels, a floating light). One to three of the household are home, busy at something; they take you coming in as their people do (the welcoming come and greet you, the curious come close, the wary back away to the far wall, the guarded stand tall and watch), and you can talk to them. Ruins are empty and overgrown. Walk out through the doorway (or press E by it) to leave.

## Codex and upgrades

Everything you record (plants, animals, formations, settlements, field notes, worlds you arrive at and radio sources you hear) goes into one **codex** for the whole galaxy (`codex.mjs`, saved as `elsewhere-codex-v1`). Each entry keeps the traits that matter (body plan, diet, biome, chemistry and so on), and older per-system saves are copied in on first load.

The codex drives the **upgrade line** (`upgrades.mjs`). There is one main line of upgrades in three chapters named after the ship (Kestrel, Petrel, Albatross), and each step upgrades the ship, the suit or your knowledge. Optional side upgrades (scanner, vehicles, extra suit gear) hang off the line. The next main step is always being researched, and you can pick one side upgrade to research alongside it. Only research can be installed, so nothing unlocks by accident; scans made earlier count. Tasks are written as things to do ("Scan a plant on a Desert world"), and for each one `objectives.mjs` finds the nearest world that would move it forward: here, elsewhere in the system, or one or two jumps away. **Show on map** opens the star map there. Press **U** or the ⬡ button for the upgrade screen, a picture of the line where you can tap any upgrade for its card. To install, hold its button until the bar fills. The panel at the top right shows the research in progress, with the nearest place for its next task. Working effects today: Suit plating, Orbital survey (worlds of systems in jump range on the map), Jump drive II/III (16 and 24 ly), Thermal lining (against the cold and heat hazard on foot), Heat plating (slower hull heating; landing beside lava), the Petrel's splashdown on water, Signal decoder, and the side upgrades Field glasses (hold Z to zoom and scan from four times as far), Long-range receiver, Life sonar, Night filter, Deep core sampler, Return beacons and Behaviour logger. The others are on the roadmap in `PROGRESSION.md`. Effects read only whether an upgrade is installed (the readers in `upgrades.mjs`), so the line can be rearranged freely.

**Test mode.** Add `?dev` to the URL (it stays on across jumps and reloads; `?dev=0` turns it off). A DEV tab appears at the left edge (or press <kbd>°</kbd>): tick any upgrade to install or remove it at once, *Install all* / *Remove all*, *Document this world* (records everything on the world you are on, so it counts as fully documented for Return beacons and tasks), and a list of the nearest worlds to try each effect on (life, stalkers, cold, hot, lava seas, water seas, a settlement), which reloads the game there. Your own save is copied aside the first time test mode changes it; *Restore my save* puts it back. Code: `dev-panel.js`.

## Check

`node --test galaxy-explorer/*.test.mjs`

Tests cover motion stability, descent limits, landing easing, seed determinism, body spacing, cruise paths, stellar physics, an Earth-like sanity check, physical consistency (water state vs temperature, no life without water), galaxy-wide variety, giant physics and placement (moons clear of the cloud tops, nothing crowding a giant's moon system), seamless giant cloud maps and ring gaps, and orbits over time (radii and heights kept, outer planets slower, speeds capped, no collisions or crowding at any time), and interstellar frames (axes match the map, worlds stay inside the system edge, stars look the same size from both sides of the edge), and ecology (food webs follow diet and size, dim worlds favour the night, hunters catch prey that later return, grazers eat plants and animals drink without walking into the sea, herds follow you across the world, animals sleep outside their hours and wake, and the ecosystem costs under 2 ms a frame), intelligence (the most advanced species builds in its body family's cultures, and only species with hands build houses and rockets; settlements stand on dry ground clear of the site and within walking range, grow with level and house their builders; planet-shapers spread across the world within the vertex budget; satellites stay in orbit; rocks, plants and formations are cleared from settlements), civilizations as named entities and radio signals (sources are real, heard within range, the same in slices or in one pass), geology (2–4 fitting kinds per world, on dry ground all over the world, a few near the site, clear of the site itself), plants (grow all over the world, not just at the site; the same plants in the same places when you come back; several biomes per world), scanning (the nearest unrecorded thing in reach, never one already recorded; clean cards), danger (stalkers notice you, lunge once or twice and never while you are aboard; intelligent species never stalk), and life rarity on the map. The browser playtest covers map selection, travel, landing, and surface interaction.
